## ABSTRACT 

Can simple learning rules keep their regret bounded in self-play? Recent work achieves constant regret bounds through modified regularization and higher-order prediction (Liu et al., 2026; Abbadi et al., 2026). Yet for Optimistic Hedge, ar-guably the most canonical method in games, the best known individual regret bound remains logarithmic (Ha, 2026). In this work, we prove that plain Optimistic Hedge with a constant step size can attain On,d (1) individual regret in general-sum games with n players and d = ( d1, . . . , d n) actions, under expected loss-vector feedback. As a corollary, its time-averaged play enjoys an On,d (1 /T ) coarse correlated equi-librium (CCE) gap. Our analysis represents Optimistic Hedge as a real-analytic recurrence on a compact space, which yields an exact finite-order difference relation that eliminates horizon dependence. Our proof hinges on nonconstructive Noethe-rianity argument of Frisch (1967), so the (n, d )-dependence remains implicit. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

A central question in game theory (von Neumann & Morgenstern, 1944; Nash, 1950) is whether independent learning can lead to an equilibrium when opponents are adapting. Such interdependent learning dynamics underpin modern applications such as poker self-play (Brown & Sandholm, 2019), adversarial training (Goodfellow et al., 2014), and game-theoretic approaches to language-model alignment (Munos et al., 2024; Rosset et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2024; Gölz et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2026). No-regret learning (Cesa-Bianchi & Lugosi, 2006) offers a decentralized approach: players seek low regret against the best fixed action in hindsight (Hannan, 1957). Indeed, sublinear external regret drives time-averaged play toward an equilibrium (Moulin & Vial, 1978; Hart & Mas-Colell, 2001). While classical online learning guarantees O(√T ) regret against arbitrary losses (Freund & Schapire, 1997), self-play adds an additional structure: a common update rule shared by all players. Optimistic methods exploit this structure and use recent losses to predict the next one and improve regret bounds (Rakhlin & Sridharan, 2013a; Syrgkanis et al., 2015). Optimistic Hedge, the most canonical optimistic method for games, has recently been shown to enjoy polylogarithmic (Daskalakis et al., 2021) and logarithmic (Ha, 2026) regret bounds. On the other hand, recent alternatives have shown that modified regularization and higher-order prediction methods can produce horizon-independent regret bounds (Liu et al., 2026; Abbadi et al., 2026). In this paper, we ask the following natural question: 

Can Optimistic Hedge attain horizon-independent individual regret in general-sum games? 

Contributions. Our main result gives an affirmative answer. Our contributions are two-fold. (i) An analytic finite-order difference relation. Based on the high-order smoothness framework (Daskalakis et al., 2021) and the weighted-gap reduction (Ha, 2026), we represent Optimistic Hedge as an analytic recurrence on a compact domain. A Noetherianity argument then yields an exact finite-order difference relation controlling the first-order weighted-gap differences. (ii) A horizon-independent regret bound. Based on the finite-order difference relation and a simple finite-difference interpolation argument, we obtain On,d (1) individual regret and an On,d (1 /T )

CCE gap. The dimension dependence remains nonconstructive. Section 2 reviews prior work; Sections 3–4 introduce the setting and main result. Section 5 explains the proof strategy, with detailed proofs in the appendices. 1arXiv:2609.22839v1 [cs.GT] 19 Sep 2026 2 RELATED WORK 

No-regret learning and equilibrium. The link between no-regret learning and equilibrium computa-tion dates back to Blackwell (1956) and Hannan (1957). External regret compares cumulative loss with the best fixed action in hindsight. When all players achieve sublinear regret, averaging yields approximate Nash equilibria in two-player zero-sum games (Freund & Schapire, 1999) and CCE in general-sum games (Moulin & Vial, 1978; Hart & Mas-Colell, 2001). Controlling recommendation-dependent deviations via internal or swap regret yields correlated equilibrium (Hart & Mas-Colell, 2000; Blum & Mansour, 2007). 

Individual regret in self-play. Improving upon standard no-regret learning algorithms, such as follow-the-regularized-leader (FTRL), optimistic algorithms exploit predictable loss sequences (Chiang et al., 2012; Rakhlin & Sridharan, 2013a). Notably, the seminal work of Syrgkanis et al. (2015) showed that optimistic follow-the-regularized-leader (OFTRL) and optimistic mirror descent (OMD) can exploit the self-play-induced predictability in games to achieve O(√n log dmax T 1/4) individual regret, where dmax := max i di is the maximum number of actions across players. For Optimistic Hedge, Chen & Peng (2020) obtained O(log 5/6 dmax T 1/6) in two-player games, and Daskalakis et al. (2021) established O(n log di log 4 T ) in n-player games based on a high-order smoothness analysis. A recent line of work improves regret bounds by modifying regularization, step sizes, or prediction methods. Log-regularized lifted OFTRL (LRL-OFTRL) (Farina et al., 2022) enlarges the regu-larized strategy space and achieves O(nd max log T ) in finite general-sum games, and extends to general convex strategy sets. Cautious Optimistic MWU (COMWU) (Soleymani et al., 2025a;b) achieves O(n log 2 dmax log T ) through adaptive step sizes. ECHO-OFTRL (Liu et al., 2026) and HOOD (Abbadi et al., 2026) combine higher-order prediction with lifted regularization to obtain 

O(n21 log 4 dmax ) and O(n3 log 2 dmax ) regret bounds, respectively. Table 1 compares these individ-ual regret bounds. 

Other regret notions and update rules. A parallel line studies swap regret to obtain guarantees for correlated equilibrium (CE) (Aumann, 1974). Anagnostides et al. (2022a) extend the high-order smoothness framework of Daskalakis et al. (2021) to obtain polylogarithmic internal and swap regret. Log-barrier regularization (Anagnostides et al., 2022b) and hybrid regularization (Tsuchiya, 2026) yield logarithmic and sublogarithmic swap regret, respectively. Clairvoyant Multiplicative Weights Update (CMWU) (Piliouras et al., 2022) obtains horizon-independent regret via implicit updates; however, its uncoupled implementation guarantees this bound only on a selected subsequence. For two-player general-sum games, Tao & Zheng (2026) achieve O(log dmax ) alternating regret and 

O(log dmax /T ) CCE error using sequential updates, unlike our simultaneous external-regret setting. 

Closest prior work. The closest works are Daskalakis et al. (2021) and Ha (2026). We build on the high-order smoothness framework of Daskalakis et al. (2021) and the recurrence-based weighted-gap analysis of Ha (2026). In particular, Ha (2026) represents Optimistic Hedge as a recurrent map acting on a state space equipped with a fixed Euclidean norm. Crucially, the high-order estimates of Ha (2026) leave a remainder that grows with the horizon at a fixed step size. In this work, we instead obtain an exact relation among difference orders and eliminate the horizon dependence in our final regret bound.

## 6 DISCUSSION 

Implications. Notably, a recent line of work has obtained horizon-independent regret bounds in finite general-sum games by combining modified regularization with higher-order prediction (Liu et al., 2026; Abbadi et al., 2026). However, Theorem 4.1 shows that plain Optimistic Hedge can already attain this horizon independence with a dimension-dependent constant step size. Our result suggests that adaptive learning rates (Soleymani et al., 2025a;b), hybrid regularization (Tsuchiya, 2026), and higher-order predictions (Liu et al., 2026; Abbadi et al., 2026) are unnecessary for this guarantee. 

Comparison with Ha (2026). Both our analysis and the analysis of Ha (2026) build on the high-order smoothness framework of Daskalakis et al. (2021). Ha’s centered-logit derivative bounds control each difference order separately. Choosing m ≍ (√nη )−1 leaves a regret contribution proportional to 

ηT exp {− c/ (√nη )}, leading to a logarithmic regret bound when the step size is tuned to the horizon. In our analysis, Frisch’s theorem on the compact state space yields an exact relation among difference orders (Lemma 5.8). Its order is fixed by (n, d ), and it has no additive error that accumulates over 

T . Finite-difference interpolation (Lemma 5.4) then yields a first-order difference bound that lets us absorb prediction error into stability, up to a horizon-independent error. Intuitively, our approach thus trades Ha’s explicit dimension dependence for horizon independence. 

## 7 CONCLUSION 

In this work, we proved that plain Optimistic Hedge with a constant step size can achieve horizon-independent individual regret in finite general-sum games. An exact finite-order relation for weighted gaps removes the horizon-accumulating remainder in the logarithmic analysis of Ha (2026), and yields an On,d (1 /T ) CCE guarantee. Frisch’s theorem lets us select finitely many analytic generators near a compact semianalytic set. We introduce the definitions and three classical analytic lemmas needed for its proof. We state these lemmas in terms of scalar functions and analytic coefficients; their analytic foundations are supplied by the cited results. We reproduce the proof of Frisch’s theorem using these lemmas. 

Definition C.1 (Real-analytic functions) . Let U ⊂ Rq be open. A function f : U → R is real-analytic if, for every x ∈ U , f (x′) equals a convergent power series in x′ − x for x′ in a neighborhood of x.

Definition C.2 (Analytic germs) . Two real-analytic functions, each defined on an open neighborhood of a compact set K ⊂ Rq , represent the same germ along K if they agree on an open neighborhood of K contained in both domains. A germ along K is an equivalence class under this relation. The set of these germs is denoted by O(K).

Intuitively, O(K) describes real-analytic functions near K, identifying two functions whenever they agree on an open neighborhood of K. In particular, O(K) forms a commutative ring: the sum and product of germs represented by f and g are represented by f + g and f g on the intersection of their domains. When K = {x}, write Ox := O({x}) and denote the germ of f at x by fx. Note that agreement only at the points of K, rather than on a neighborhood of K, does not identify germs. 

Definition C.3 (Ideals) . A subset I of a commutative ring R is an ideal if it contains zero and is closed under addition and multiplication by arbitrary elements of R.
