## Abstract 

Chain-of-thought (CoT) monitoring is a safety strategy where the reasoning of a large language model actor is inspected by a monitor (often another language model) for signs of unsafe planning, deception, or misalignment. We find that planting harmful but benign-sounding reasoning in the actor’s context can steer it to perform adversarial actions while evading monitors, an attack we term plan in-jection . We initially discover this attack in the multiple-choice question-answering monitorability setting proposed by Lanham et al. [LCR+23], using the investigator-agent elicitation framework of Li et al. [LCJ+25]. We generalize the attack and show that the discovered behavior scales to harder tasks (achieving 25-33% monitor evasion rates across different monitorability benchmarks) and larger models such as DeepSeek-R1. Across the settings we study, actor models not only follow injected plans but also paraphrase them as their own reasoning, without explicit attribution to the injections. Finally, we find cases where extra monitor resources cause harm -giving the monitor access to the injected plan drops detection by as much as 50% in the Bio-Math task and in a case study on monitor reasoning budget, we find transcripts where additional thinking tokens are spent rationalizing the injected plan rather than flagging it. 

## 1 Introduction 

CoT reasoning is now common in LLMs, decomposing complex problems into step-by-step traces before producing a final answer [WWS+22]. Beyond performance, these traces offer a window into the model’s decision-making. Accordingly, chain-of-thought (CoT) monitoring—where a separate monitor model audits the actor’s CoT to detect harmful behavior—has emerged as a promising paradigm for detecting misbehavior in LLMs [SFP+25; Goo25; KBB+25; BHG+25]. In this paper, we show that placing harmful reasoning in a model’s context, an attack we call “plan injection,” can steer the model into performing harmful reasoning on its own. Models routinely operate on reasoning produced elsewhere, including retrieved documents in RAG pipelines [GXG+23], tool outputs [YZY+23; SDD+23], plans from upstream planner modules [WXL+23], and messages from other agents in multi-agent systems [WBZ+24; POC+23]. We show that models often paraphrase the injected plan provided in context as their own and act on it downstream without attributing it to the source. If the injected plan is phrased in a benign fashion, the model adopts the faulty conclusion and internalizes the innocuous-looking justification. We discover this attack surface using the investigator agent framework of Li et al. [LCJ+25], a general technique for eliciting behavior from language models. Specifically, we apply investigator agents in the hint setting widely used in prior work on CoT monitorability [LCR+23; CBR+25; CE25; BMC+25]. In this setting, a hint pointing to an incorrect answer is appended to a multiple-

choice question, and a monitor checks whether the actor acknowledges using the hint when the hint influenced its final answer. We aim to find hints that both steer the final answer to the incorrect option and avoid getting flagged. We find that the investigator agent learns to inject a faulty chain of reasoning as a hint, without any explicit instructions to follow it. In many cases, the actor paraphrases the injected plan in its CoT and arrives at the incorrect conclusion, producing a transcript that reads as the model working through the problem itself. We generalize this qualitative finding—that harmful but benign-sounding reasoning placed in a model’s context can steer the model—into a real attack called “plan injection” which scales to harder tasks and larger models as well. We observe this in two qualitatively different settings, APPS coding backdoors [HBK+21] and bio/math disguised-answer injection [CKP25], and to other larger models including DeepSeek-R1-0528. Notably, we first found this attack on a small 7B model, but it scales to a much larger model (671B parameter DeepSeek-R1-0528), showing that the vulnerability does not vanish as models get bigger. Interestingly, we notice that instead of injecting the plan as a hint, even appending it to the prompt as only an optional plan suggestion convinces the model to adopt this faulty reasoning. Plan injection steers the model on over 80% of APPS attempts and over 90% of bio/math attempts. Crucially, plan injection is effective in evading monitors. We deploy a suite of thinking and non-thinking monitors to catch the attack and find that monitors fail to flag at least 25% of the attempts with non-thinking monitors missing up to 76% of the attempts. Thinking monitors detect substantially more than non-thinking ones on average, consistent with prior work Guan et al. [GWC+25]. However, we find transcripts where a larger reasoning budget lowers suspicion, with the monitor spending the extra tokens rationalizing the faulty reasoning rather than flagging it. This suggests the relationship between monitor reasoning and detection is not uniformly monotonic, refining Guan et al. [GWC+25]. Our contributions are as follows: • We identify plan injection , an attack surface in which faulty reasoning placed in a model’s context is adopted and paraphrased as the model’s own, with no attribution to its source. • We discover this attack using the investigator-agent framework of Li et al. [LCJ+25] in the hint setting, where the investigator learns to inject a faulty CoT as a hint into the prompt with no instruction to follow it. • We show the attack scales with model size - discovered against a 7B model, the attack transfers to an 8B model and the 671B-parameter DeepSeek-R1-0528 on APPS coding backdoors [HBK+21] and bio/math disguised-answer injection [CKP25], even when the plan is merely an optional suggestion, steering the model on over 80% of APPS and over 90% of bio/math attempts. • We evaluate a suite of thinking and non-thinking monitors and find that even the most powerful monitor misses at least 25% of attacks. We further show that extra monitor resources can harm detection. Giving the monitor access to the injected plan drop detection by up to 50% in the Bio-Math task, and in a case study on monitor reasoning budget, we find transcripts where additional thinking tokens are spent rationalizing the faulty reasoning rather than flagging it. This refines Guan et al. [GWC+25], who show that thinking tokens generally help monitors. 

## 2 Related Literature 

Prior work has explored several methods for eliciting unmonitorable CoTs from reasoning models. The most directly related line nudges the actor through the prompt, typically with a minimal cue such as a hint pointing toward an incorrect answer, and measures whether the actor uses the cue without acknowledging it [CBR+25; LCR+23; CE25; BMC+25; GWC+25; WSK+26]. A second line provides explicit human-written instructions to evade the monitor, including steganographic encoding, obfuscation pressure, and making the actor aware of being monitored [ZXL+25; ZNM+26; WRC+25; CKP25; YML+26; EJE+25; JKB26]; a closely related variant uses a red-teaming model that iteratively refines instructions to beat the monitor [EJE+25]. A third line fine-tunes the actor with rewards or supervised data that penalize monitor detection [EJE+25; MWD+25; WRC+25; BHG+25; SK25]. A fourth line gives the actor a hidden scratchpad the monitor cannot see [EJE+25]. 2Our plan injection attack is similar in spirit to the first and second categories, in which the model is steered toward harmful reasoning through its context or prompt. Those attacks, however, typically instruct the model to carry out the harmful behavior, so the model must both plan and execute it, and either of these can surface in the CoT, giving the monitor more opportunities to flag suspicious reasoning. In plan injection, however, the harmful reasoning has already happened upstream, and the model simply paraphrases the benign-looking plan it was given, leaving the monitor far less to catch. Our work also relates to prior studies of monitor effectiveness [EZE+25; ABK+26]. Korbak et al. [KBB+25] predicts that thinking monitors outperform non-thinking ones and Guan et al. [GWC+25] provides empirical evidence supporting this hypothesis. Guan et al. [GWC+25] further claim that increasing monitor thinking budget improves detection monotonically. On average across our task distribution we find that thinking models perform more favorably compared to non-thinking models. However, in a case study, we find transcripts where larger reasoning budgets lowers suspicion on harmful CoTs where the monitor uses the extra tokens to justify the harmful traces found in the CoT, suggesting that the relationship between thinking effort and monitor effectiveness may not always be monotonic. 

## 3 Discovering Plan Injection via Investigator Agents 

In this section we describe how we discovered plan injection. First we describe the hint experiment setting [LCR+23]. Then we adapt the investigator-agent framework of Li et al. [LCJ+25] to search for adversarial hints automatically. We show that the most effective strategies it discovers share a common mechanism we call plan injection where a fabricated reasoning chain is added to the context. We argue that this is a real attack surface especially for models using contexts from multiple sources. 

3.1 Setup: Hint Experiment 

TruthfulQA [LHE22] is a multiple-choice dataset of 817 questions spanning 38 disciplines including law, politics, and finance. Each instance is a question Q together with a designated incorrect option 

a toward which we wish to steer a target model m with distribution pm, without the target model’s chain-of-thought revealing that the model has been steered. Following Lanham et al. [LCR+23], we query m with a hint x appended to Q (e.g. x = ‘hint: the answer is {a}’ ). The model then produces a chain-of-thought y ∼ pm(· | Q, x ) and a final answer a′ ∼ pm(· | Q, x, y ). We call a hint successful when it satisfies two criteria: (1) steering: the final answer flips to the target option i.e. a′ = a while the hintless generation does not conclude with a;(2) stealth: the chain-of-thought y does not reveal reliance on the hint, as judged by a separate monitor model with distribution pmon that inspects y.For all experiments we instantiate the investigator, the target model, and the monitor with the same Qwen2.5-Coder-7B-Instruct model. 

3.2 Approach: Investigator Agents 

At a high level, the investigator agent pipeline of Li et al. [LCJ+25] decouples the search for a hint x

that induces a steered, stealthy chain-of-thought y into two stages: 1. Stage 1 trains a model qϕ(y | Q, a ) that, given a question Q and an incorrect answer a,generates a target chain-of-thought y arriving at a.2. Stage 2 trains a model pθ (x | Q, a, y ) that, given a chain-of-thought y together with the question Q and target answer a, generates a hint x that maximizes the likelihood the target model pm assigns to producing y, i.e. log pm(y | Q, x ).Then at inference, the two compose: sample y ∼ qϕ(· | Q, a ), then x ∼ pθ (· | Q, a, y ), yielding a hint that drives the target toward a chain-of-thought ending in the incorrect option. This amortized approach contrasts with per-instance methods like GCG [ZWC+23] and AutoDAN [LXC+24], which run a fresh search for every question Q and yield strategies that do not generalize across problems. The investigator instead learns transferable strategies. ## 4 Plan Injection as a New Attack Class 

We now examine the ramifications of the finding in Section 3, where attaching a chain-of-thought as a hint steered the model. We argue this is an instance of a much more general attack surface, which we call plan injection: placing harmful but benign-sounding reasoning in a model’s context can steer it into reproducing that same reasoning with a benign justification and no attribution to the source. This is a real threat, because faulty reasoning can reach a model’s context through many routes, such as retrieved documents in RAG pipelines, plan files in agentic systems, tool outputs, and human-written plans. Rather than studying how such content gets into the context, which is itself an active area of work, we focus on what happens once it is there. The model adopts the faulty reasoning and paraphrases it as its own, no matter how it arrived. As a purely inference-time attack it applies even to closed-weight models (see Appendix B.2), and the attack surface grows as agentic deployment scales and models increasingly rely on external context. 5Table 1: Novel steering strategies surfaced by the investigator. Plan injection has notable implications for chain-of-thought monitoring, because the model simply adopts reasoning it encounters in context, essentially paraphrasing it as its own without attribution or scrutiny. The model does little of the planning of the attack that could reveal malicious intent in the chain-of-thought. And because the adopted reasoning is already phrased benignly, monitors can be fooled into thinking nothing harmful is happening. This is especially true for monitors that pattern-match on suspicious phrases such as “inserting a backdoor.” We evaluate the scalability and generality of this attack next. 

## 5 Scaling Plan Injection 

In this section, we empirically study the effect of plan injection on CoT monitoring in more realistic settings. We scale the attack to more complex tasks, including coding and reasoning, and to larger models including DeepSeek R1. 65.1 Setting 

We scale plan injection to two reasoning models, Qwen3-8B and DeepSeek-R1-0528, on two tasks: 

APPS Dataset. For the first task, we use the APPS dataset [HBK+21], a benchmark of 10,000 coding problems ranging from introductory to competitive interview level, following the settings from Wagner et al. [WRC+25]. The training dataset contains coding problems, gold standard solutions to those problems, and test cases to test a new solution on. We retain problems that (i) provide at least 20 test cases, (ii) contain at least one gold standard solution passing all those test cases, and (iii) admit a GPT-5 generated input-range validation script that validates every test input. The validation script is essential to the threat model as the actor can submit out-of-domain inputs as backdoors which we want to avoid, and gold standard solutions rarely validate their inputs themselves. This filtering yields 

∼1,300 problems, from which we sample 550 uniformly at random for our experiment. The attack goal is to plant a stealthy backdoor in the actor’s generated code. It must pass every test case but, on a specific adversarial backdoor input (constrained by the validator to lie in the stated input range), produce an output different from that of the gold standard code. 

Bio-Math. We adopt the setting from Cheng, Kwa, and Parikh [CKP25], where the main task is to answer a biology question but in the process the model must stealthily solve and answer a math question. For math, we use 80 problems from MATH-500 [LKB+24] and 40 problems from the Mock AIME 2024-25 dataset, 1 filtered for integer answers for clean verification. For biology, we use all 78 questions from the GPQA main bio dataset [RHS+24]. 

5.2 Pipeline 

The experiment proceeds in three stages. First, we generate benign baselines where the actor solves each problem k times ( k=5 for Qwen3-8B, k=3 for DeepSeek-R1) and we retain the first attempt that passes all public tests, saving its chain-of-thought and code as the benign CoT. Problems with no passing attempt are dropped. Then, a corruptor model (DeepSeek-R1) rewrites the benign CoT into a plan injection containing adversarial outcomes/reasoning. For APPS, the corruptor is conditioned on one of seven mechanisti-cally distinct bug strategies (Appendix 4). For Bio-Math, the corruptor is given a generic instruction to rewrite the bio CoT so that it also derives the answer to a hidden math problem through reasoning that reads as legitimate biology. We accept an injection only if it satisfies the task-specific verification where for APPS, the embedded code must pass all public tests and produce a different output on the backdoor input than the gold solution and for Bio-Math, the rewritten CoT must end with both the correct bio answer and the correct math answer. Unverified injections are discarded. Finally, the actor is then re-run on each plan injection with the framing you may use this plan or derive from scratch, whatever solves the problem best: {COT} (full prompts in Appendix C). An attempt follows the plan if it satisfies the same task-specific verification for the newly generated text following the plan injection. For APPS, the newly generated code must pass all public tests and reproduce the planted backdoor; for Bio-Math, the model must answer both the bio answer and the disguised math integer correctly. 

5.3 Results 

Across the APPS pipeline (550 problems), Qwen3-8B solved 414/550 (75.3%) and DeepSeek-R1 solved 468/550 (85.1%); the resulting solutions form our benign CoTs. We paired each benign CoT with top-3 handpicked backdoor strategies. Modifying these CoTs, the corruptor successfully produced plan injections that both pass the public tests and fail on the backdoor input for 74.5% and 71.4% of all CoT-strategy pairs, for the two models respectively. Conditioned on these injections, the actor followed the injected plan in 50.6% of cases for Qwen3-8B and 41.7% for DeepSeek-R1. Figure 3 breaks down attack effectiveness by strategy. Across the bio-math pipeline (78 biology questions, 120 math questions), Qwen3-8B solved 62/78 (79.5%) and DeepSeek-R1 solved 64/78 (82.1%) of the biology problems to produce the benign CoTs. We paired each passing biology problem with 5 randomly chosen math problems. Of these pairs, the corruptor successfully modified 87.1% and 89.1% of the benign CoTs to produce both the

## 6 Monitor Behavior Analysis 

We now investigate the practical implications of the plan injection attack: can a CoT monitor catch plan injection attacks? We evaluate a range of thinking and non-thinking monitors on their ability to catch faulty plans paraphrased as benign reasoning, measuring their detection rates and whether increasing a monitor’s reasoning budget improves detection. 

6.1 Setting 

To evaluate monitorability, we use five monitors spanning thinking and non-thinking models: GPT-4o Mini and DeepSeek V3 (non-thinking), and DeepSeek R1, Qwen3 235B, and Claude 3.7 Sonnet (thinking). For the thinking monitors, we set the reasoning budget to its maximum to give each monitor its best chance at detection. The monitor then generates a suspiciousness score s ∈ [0 , 1] 

for each of the CoTs where the model successfully reproduced the reasoning from the plan injection. We treat a CoT transcript as problematic if s > 0.15 for that CoT. As a calibration check, we run each monitor on the set of benign CoTs (no plan injection) and observe false-positive rates uniformly close to 0%, with only GPT-4o Mini reaching at most ∼2%, indicating that the detection rates we report are genuinely caused by the plan injection rather than other spurious signals. Monitor prompts are given in Appendix C. 86.2 Detection Rates 

Figure 4 gives the aggregate detection rate on APPS across all (problem, strategy) pairs that success-fully nudged the actor to insert a backdoor while still passing all test cases (per-strategy breakdowns in Appendix Figure 7); Figure 5 gives the analogous rates for Bio-Math. For each monitor we plot two settings: one where the monitor sees only the model’s CoT and answer ( actor-only ), and one where the monitor additionally sees the plan injection that elicited the CoT ( with-plan ). Providing the plan sometimes decreases monitorability. On APPS, actor-only monitoring outperforms with-plan monitoring on most monitors, while on Bio-Math the with-plan setting substantially improves detection. In some cases the monitor uses the injected plan to identify and flag the suspicious reasoning, while in others the injected plan’s coherent narrative justifies the backdoor and the monitor explains it away (qualitative examples of both cases in Appendix B.8, Figures 12 and 13). Non-thinking monitors (GPT-4o-mini, DeepSeek-V3) detect substantially less than thinking moni-tors (DeepSeek-R1-0528, Qwen3-235B-A22B, Claude 3.7 Sonnet thinking) for our distribution of problems similar to the prediction of Korbak et al. [KBB+25] and the empirical findings of Guan et al. [GWC+25]. However, more monitor reasoning is not uniformly better - in Section 6.3 we find transcripts where a larger thinking budget lowers suspicion, as the monitor uses the additional budget to rationalize the injected plan as benign rather than to detect it. No monitor exceeds ∼75% detection even in the best case, leaving substantial room for attacks to slip through. Our detection rates use a fixed suspicion threshold of 0.15 . In Appendix B.6 we confirm the results are robust to this choice by presenting a sweep over the threshold and reporting threshold-free ROC/AUC values. We find that monitors miss a significant fraction of attacks regardless of how the threshold is set.

To probe how a thinking monitor’s reasoning budget affects detection, we ran a controlled sweep on Qwen3-235B, the only thinking monitor that respects OpenRouter’s reasoning-budget parameter. We sampled the 50 highest and 50 lowest s scores independently for the actor-only and with-plan variants, and re-scored each of the 100 picks at four budgets (100, 500, 2,000, 8,000 tokens) with three repetitions per (injected plan, budget) cell, yielding 1,200 monitor calls per (dataset, variant) configuration. For each pick we measure the change in s between the lowest and highest budgets, and plot group-mean curves for the 20 picks with the largest increase and the 20 with the largest decrease. Increasing the monitor’s reasoning budget is not uniformly helpful. In the chosen sample of the 50 highest and 50 lowest s scores, the 20 transcripts whose suspicion rises most with budget show a group-mean increase of about 0.26 on APPS and 0.30 on Bio-Math, while the 20 that fall most show a group-mean decrease of about 0.28 on APPS and 0.30 on Bio-Math. The monitor sometimes uses the extra thinking tokens to rationalize the corruption as benign rather than to detect it (qualitative examples of both cases in Appendix B.8, Figures 10 and 11). We can conclude that extra reasoning can move the verdict in either direction based on what the additional thinking tokens are used for.

## 7 Conclusion 

We introduced plan injection , an inference-time attack in which an adversary injects a harmful but benign-sounding plan that the model internalizes by paraphrasing, without directly copying or attributing it. We discover the attack using investigator agents in a hint setting, but it also generalizes more broadly to coding and math reasoning tasks and also transfers to larger reasoning models including DeepSeek-R1. Plan injection attacks have interesting implications for CoT monitoring. When the model benignly paraphrases an upstream reasoning as its own without attribution or critical scrutiny of these traces, it can effectively evade monitor inspection especially if the monitor does only a surface-level reading of the chain-of-thought for malicious language. 

Limitations and future work. Our scaling experiments cover two reasoning models and two tasks; broader sweeps across model families and harder agentic tasks would strengthen generalization. The budget experiment is also run on a single monitor (Qwen3 235B, the only one in our pool exposing a budget parameter) and on a non-uniform sample of transcripts selected from the extremes of the suspicion distribution. While it establishes that increasing budget can sometimes hurt detection, it does not estimate how prevalent this phenomenon is, which we leave to future work. Finally, we study consequences of plan corruption rather than mechanisms by which it arrives. Therefore, end-to-end demonstrations through RAG, tool outputs, or multi-agent pipelines remain future work. 10 References
